The 1-on-1 Meeting Template That Actually Works

Bad 1-on-1s waste hours every week. Here's the structure that turns them into the most useful meeting on your calendar.

Most managers run 1-on-1s the same way: open with "how's it going?" — drift into status updates — wrap up. The meetings feel pleasant and produce nothing. Reports leave wondering whether anything they said registered.

The fix is structure. Same questions every meeting. Predictable cadence. Visible follow-through.

The 3 questions for every FIRST 1-on-1

If you're a new manager — or just starting 1-on-1s with a new direct report — open with these three questions, in this order.

  1. "What's working that I shouldn't change?"
    This protects what's already healthy. Most new managers break things they didn't realize were load-bearing.
  2. "What's broken that nobody is naming?"
    This surfaces problems below the line of formality. Patterns nobody wrote up. Stuff people complain about in DMs but not in meetings.
  3. "What do you need from me that you didn't get from the last manager?"
    This makes their agenda visible and signals you intend to fill gaps, not repeat them.

That's it. That's the meeting. You'll learn more in 30 minutes of these three questions than in 30 hours of small talk.

The 1-on-1 agenda template (recurring)

After the first 1-on-1, settle into a recurring structure. 30 minutes is plenty for most reports. 45 if scope is big.

Minutes 0–5: Their agenda

"What's on your mind today?" Start with what THEY want to talk about. Their topics get priority. Yours come second.

Minutes 5–15: Their work

"What are you working on this week? Anything blocked?" Don't turn this into a status update — that's what tickets are for. Use it to surface where they're stuck or where you need to clear obstacles.

Minutes 15–22: Coaching / development

"What's something you're trying to get better at?" Or: "How did [recent project] go from your side?" Use this segment to give them feedback (specific, behavior-anchored) and to ask for feedback yourself.

Minutes 22–28: Your agenda

Things you need to convey, decisions to make together, context to share. Keep it short. Save broadcast topics for team meetings.

Minutes 28–30: Action items

"Who's doing what by when?" Capture in shared notes. Carry forward to the next 1-on-1.

Cadence rules

  • Weekly for direct reports new to your team or new to their role. They need predictable touchpoints.
  • Bi-weekly for established team members in stable roles.
  • Never cancel. Reschedule if something urgent comes up, but don't skip. The signal "I will always make time for you" is the foundation of trust.
  • Walking 1-on-1s, video 1-on-1s, coffee 1-on-1s — all fine. The structure matters more than the venue.

The 5 mistakes that kill 1-on-1s

  1. Treating them as status updates ("what did you do this week?")
  2. Filling the time with your own talking
  3. Skipping or rescheduling them when you're busy
  4. Not capturing action items / not following through
  5. Avoiding hard topics (when there's something hard to say, the 1-on-1 is the place — not the team meeting, not Slack)

The shared notes doc

Keep a shared running notes doc with each direct report. Two columns: their topics, your topics. Both add to it during the week. The 1-on-1 works through the list. Action items get a checkbox and a date. Carry-forward items roll to next week.

This single tool — a shared notes doc — turns 1-on-1s from "another meeting" into "the most useful meeting we have."
