The 5 Mistakes Every First-Time Manager Makes

Most first-time managers crash by month four. The reason is almost always the same five mistakes — all preventable.

Mistake 1: Strategy in week one

The most common first-time-manager mistake is announcing changes before understanding the team. New managers feel pressure to "show leadership" early, so they restructure something, change a process, or rewrite goals in their first weeks. By the time they understand the team — usually month two — they've already locked themselves into decisions they can't undo without losing face.

The fix: Listen for 30 days. Strategy in month three is informed and lasts. Strategy in week one is guessing.

Mistake 2: Avoiding the underperformer

There is always one. Every team has a member whose work is below standard, whose performance hasn't been addressed by the previous manager, and whom everyone else is quietly tired of carrying. New managers see them in week one and avoid the conversation for 60–90 days because it feels too hard too soon.

What happens next: the rest of the team loses faith in the new manager's judgment. "If they can't see this, they can't see anything."

The fix: Have the first hard conversation by day 60. Not the firing conversation — the "I noticed [behavior], what was going on?" conversation. Document. Set expectations. Offer support.

Mistake 3: Managing former peers as if nothing changed

The peer-to-manager transition is a one-way door for the relationship. New managers who pretend nothing changed end up with neither the friendship nor the management authority. The team loses trust because the new manager won't hold former friends accountable. The former friends lose the relationship because feedback comes out of nowhere when it finally arrives.

The fix: Have the reset conversation in week 1. The script: "I want to keep our friendship. I also want to do this job well. Our work relationship has to look different — and I need your help making it work."

Mistake 4: Giving vague feedback to avoid conflict

"Great job." "Keep it up." "Just a few small things to work on." First-time managers default to vague feedback because specific feedback feels confrontational. The result: nobody actually knows what good looks like, performance doesn't improve, and the next conversation has to be twice as hard.

The fix: Use specific, behavior-anchored language. "In Wednesday's meeting, when you cut Sarah off twice, the discussion lost momentum. Next time, try waiting until she finishes before responding." That's feedback. "You need to be a better listener" is not.

Mistake 5: Skipping the alignment conversation with their boss

New managers spend hours preparing for 1-on-1s with their team and zero hours preparing for 1-on-1s with their own boss. Then they're surprised in month two when their priorities don't match what they're actually being measured on.

The fix: First week, ask your boss: "What does success look like at 30, 60, 90 days? What does failure look like? What is broken that you need me to fix?" Repeat at every milestone.

The pattern

Notice the pattern: every one of these mistakes is about avoiding a hard conversation in the short term — with the team, with the underperformer, with the former peer, with the boss. New managers who survive their first 90 days are the ones who lean into those conversations early, with structure. The ones who don't, end up rebuilding from scratch in month six.
