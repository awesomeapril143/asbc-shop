How to Get WOSB Certified: A Step-by-Step Guide

By a certified WOSB & EDWOSB owner. The full process — from eligibility to submitted application.

$30+ billion every year is set aside specifically for women-owned small businesses by the U.S. federal government. To compete for any of it, you need SBA WOSB or EDWOSB certification. The application is brutal if you don't know the language. It's straightforward if you do.

Here's the full process, in order.

Step 1: Confirm eligibility (before anything else)

Don't start the application until you've confirmed:

  • Your business is a small business under SBA size standards for your primary NAICS code
  • At least 51% directly and unconditionally owned by one or more women who are U.S. citizens
  • A woman owner holds the highest officer position AND manages day-to-day operations
  • That woman owner has unconditional control over long-term and day-to-day decisions

If you also want EDWOSB (which unlocks more contracts), add: Personal Net Worth under $850K, AGI averaged over 3 years under $400K, total assets under $6.5M.

Take 5 minutes to verify with the free eligibility checker before investing time in the application.

Step 2: Register on SAM.gov (prerequisite)

SAM.gov registration is required before WOSB. If you don't have a UEI (Unique Entity Identifier), start at sam.gov. Allow 30–60 days for full validation. The registration is free.

Step 3: Gather your documents

SBA will ask for:

  • Articles of Organization / Incorporation
  • Operating Agreement or Bylaws (current and signed)
  • Most recent 3 years of business and personal tax returns
  • IRS Form 4506-T (signed)
  • Personal Net Worth statement (EDWOSB only)
  • Stock certificates and ownership ledger (if applicable)
  • Trust documents (if business is owned through a trust)
  • State business registration / good standing certificate
  • Active SAM.gov registration printout

The single biggest time-saver: review your Operating Agreement BEFORE submitting. SBA looks for "unconditional ownership and control" language. Conditioned voting rights, time-limited transfer clauses, or required co-signatures from family members are common rejection triggers.

Step 4: Open the application at certify.sba.gov

Go to certify.sba.gov, sign in with your Login.gov account (create one if needed), and start a WOSB application.

The system will ask you which certification you want. Apply for both WOSB and EDWOSB if you qualify for EDWOSB. Same form, same fee (free), strictly more contracts unlocked.

Step 5: Complete each section carefully

The application has six main sections. Don't rush.

  1. Business Information. Pull from SAM.gov to ensure consistency.
  2. Ownership. Document the 51%+ trace cleanly. If ownership flows through a holding company or trust, document every layer.
  3. Control. Reference Operating Agreement clauses that establish unconditional control by woman owner.
  4. Eligibility. Affirm size, citizenship, and other criteria.
  5. Economic Disadvantage (EDWOSB only). Personal Net Worth, AGI, total assets.
  6. Document Upload. Upload everything from Step 3.

Step 6: Submit and wait

SBA's stated processing time is roughly 90 days. Real timeline: 60–90 days for clean applications, 6–9 months for applications with gaps. Every correction request from SBA resets the clock.

The 7 most common rejection reasons

  1. Operating Agreement doesn't prove unconditional ownership and control
  2. Personal Net Worth math is calculated wrong (EDWOSB)
  3. Missing or incomplete IRS Form 4506-T
  4. Ownership trace not documented across all entities
  5. "Daily management" language is fuzzy
  6. Voting rights are conditioned
  7. Spouse or family member listed in a way that triggers control questions

Each one is fixable in advance. Each one costs you 90 days if you submit blind.

Step 7: After certification — actually winning contracts

Certification opens the door. Winning the first contract takes more. Three things help most: a sharp capability statement, an active hunt for set-aside opportunities (SAM.gov, ESBD, agency forecasts), and willingness to pursue subcontracting under primes who already have prime contracts in your NAICS.
